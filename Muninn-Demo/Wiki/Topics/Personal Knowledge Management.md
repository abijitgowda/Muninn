Personal Knowledge Management (PKM) encompasses the tools, practices, and mental models that individuals use to capture, organize, connect, and retrieve information across their personal and professional lives. The concept has roots in Vannevar Bush's 1945 essay "As We May Think," which envisioned a device called the Memex for storing and cross-referencing all of a person's books, records, and communications. Modern PKM tools — [[Obsidian]], Roam Research, Notion, Logseq — have brought this vision closer to reality with features like bidirectional linking, graph visualization, and block-level references.

The traditional challenge with PKM is maintenance burden: organizing knowledge takes time and discipline that most people lack. The [[Zettelkasten]] method, popularized by German sociologist Niklas Luhmann, demonstrated that a well-maintained slip-box of interlinked notes could become a genuine thinking partner — but Luhmann spent decades maintaining his. Tools like Obsidian reduced the friction compared to physical cards but still require the human to do the linking, tagging, and filing. This is the gap that [[Andrej Karpathy]]'s [[LLM Wiki]] concept addresses: by delegating the organizational work to a large language model, the human can focus on feeding sources while the LLM handles extraction, cross-linking, and maintenance.

The shift from human-maintained PKM to LLM-maintained PKM represents a qualitative change in what is possible. A system that ingests every article you read, every tweet you bookmark, and every PDF you save — and automatically distills them into interconnected wiki pages — can accumulate knowledge at a scale no individual could match manually. The result is a "[[Second Brain]]" that genuinely stays current, because the maintenance is automated rather than dependent on human willpower.
