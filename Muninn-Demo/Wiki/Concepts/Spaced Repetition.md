Spaced repetition is an evidence-based learning technique that schedules the review of material at progressively increasing intervals, exploiting the psychological spacing effect to maximize long-term retention while minimizing study time. The foundational research dates to Hermann Ebbinghaus's 1885 work on the forgetting curve, which demonstrated that memory decays exponentially without reinforcement. Modern spaced repetition systems (SRS) like Anki, SuperMemo, and Mnemosyne use algorithms to calculate the optimal review interval for each item based on the learner's performance history. Piotr Wozniak's SM-2 algorithm, developed in 1987, remains the basis for most SRS implementations.

The technique is particularly powerful when combined with other [[Personal Knowledge Management]] practices. A [[Zettelkasten]] provides the raw material — atomic, interlinked notes — while spaced repetition ensures that key ideas are actively reinforced rather than passively archived. [[Tiago Forte]]'s [[Second Brain]] methodology focuses more on external storage than active recall, creating a natural complementarity: the Second Brain captures and organizes everything, while spaced repetition selectively strengthens the knowledge you need to hold in biological memory. Tools like [[Obsidian]] have spaced repetition plugins that bridge these approaches, turning wiki pages into flashcard sources.

The concept of spaced repetition has interesting parallels with the memory models used in [[LLM Wiki]] systems. [[Muninn]]'s architecture includes synaptic decay and consolidation cycles that mirror the spacing effect: pages that are frequently accessed or reinforced maintain high strength scores, while neglected pages gradually decay. This biologically inspired approach to knowledge management — where the system itself "forgets" unused information and reinforces important connections — represents a convergence of cognitive science principles with AI-maintained knowledge bases.
